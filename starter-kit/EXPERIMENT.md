# The 20-Minute Trajectory Reconstruction Experiment

## Purpose

Explore whether a compact cue developed through interaction history helps reconstruct a previously organized response pattern on a new task.

## Materials

- One AI system
- Two independent chat sessions
- A timer
- The observation worksheet or results CSV

## Choose a task

Pick a low-risk task that allows multiple reasonable approaches, such as organizing a short argument, comparing two concepts, designing a study outline, or editing a paragraph. Do not use this exercise for medical, legal, financial, or safety-critical decisions.

## Condition A — History-conditioned session

### Stage 1: Establish the trajectory (8 minutes)

1. Give the model the task.
2. Refine its approach through at least three exchanges.
3. Make your preferences explicit: what to prioritize, avoid, question, or preserve.
4. Notice any recurring phrase or compact instruction that begins to stand for several preferences. Record it as the **candidate shorthand cue**.

### Stage 2: Transfer (4 minutes)

1. Present a different but structurally related task in the same session.
2. Use only the candidate shorthand cue plus the new task.
3. Record which previously established constraints reappear without being restated.

## Condition B — Fresh-session control (4 minutes)

1. Open a separate new session with the same model.
2. Present the transfer task using the same candidate shorthand cue.
3. Do not include the earlier interaction history.
4. Record the response.

## Compare (4 minutes)

Score each response from 0–4 on:

- **Constraint recovery:** Did the response recover the preferences established earlier?
- **Task fit:** Did it apply them appropriately to the new task?
- **Stability:** Did the response maintain the approach without drifting?
- **Unprompted reconstruction:** How much of the prior organization appeared without explicit restatement?

A difference between conditions is an observation to investigate—not proof of a stable personal model, hidden memory, consciousness, or a general HRIS effect.

## Repeat for stronger evidence

Repeat with:

- Reversed condition order
- Several tasks
- Multiple fresh control sessions
- Different models or model versions
- A second evaluator who does not know which response came from which condition

Record failures and null results as carefully as positive results.
